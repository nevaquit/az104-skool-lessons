The concept

RBAC answers 1 question: who can do what, and where. Every role assignment is built from exactly 3 parts:

  1. Security principal: who gets access. A user, a group, a service principal, or a managed identity. Best practice: assign to groups, never to individual users.
  2. Role definition: what they can do. A named set of permissions such as Reader, Contributor, or Owner (Day 17), or a custom role (Day 18).
  3. Scope: where it applies. A management group, subscription, resource group, or individual resource. Assignments inherit downward: access granted at the subscription flows to every resource group and resource inside it.

Permissions inside a role definition are listed as Actions (control plane operations, like creating a VM), NotActions (carved back out), DataActions (data plane operations, like reading blobs), and NotDataActions.

2 rules govern everything:

  • Permissions are additive. If you hold Reader on the subscription and Contributor on 1 resource group, you are a Contributor inside that resource group. There is no "subtract access" assignment you can create; deny assignments exist but only Azure creates them, for example through Blueprints.
  • The narrowest scope that works wins. Granting Contributor at the subscription when only 1 resource group needs it violates least privilege and will be the wrong answer on the exam.

For privileged roles, Privileged Identity Management (PIM) converts permanent assignments into eligible ones: users activate the role just in time, with approval and an expiry time, instead of holding it forever.

Key vocabulary

  • Role definition: a named set of permissions (Actions, NotActions, DataActions, NotDataActions).
  • Role assignment: principal plus role definition plus scope.
  • Security principal: the identity receiving access: user, group, service principal, or managed identity.
  • Scope: where the assignment applies; inherits downward.
  • Deny assignment: a system-created block on actions; you cannot create these directly.
  • Privileged Identity Management (PIM): just-in-time elevation for privileged roles.

Try it

You need any Azure account, including a free 1. This lab is read only. Nothing is created or changed.

In the portal:

  1. Open any resource group, then Access control (IAM).
  2. Open the Role assignments tab. Note the 3 columns that matter: principal, role, and scope. Check whether any assignments show as inherited from the subscription.
  3. Switch to the Roles tab, open Contributor, and view its JSON. Compare the actions list with NotActions to see how the role is shaped.

In the CLI (Cloud Shell is fine):

  1. List your assignments: az role assignment list --query "[].{Principal:principalName, Role:roleDefinitionName, Scope:scope}" --output table
  2. Show the Reader definition: az role definition list --query "[?roleName=='Reader'].{Role:roleName, Description:description}" --output table

Worked example: Meridian Health

Meridian designs access around groups and scopes:

  • Platform team (Entra group): Contributor on the Meridian-Prod subscription. They can manage everything in production but cannot grant access to anyone.
  • Developers (Entra group): Contributor on the Meridian-DevTest subscription only. Zero access to production.
  • Auditors (Entra group): Reader on both subscriptions. They can view everything and change nothing.
  • 2 senior admins: Owner at the management group, eligible through PIM instead of permanent, so the keys to the kingdom are only held when needed.

When a developer changes teams, Meridian updates group membership in Entra ID. No role assignment is ever touched. That is why the exam rewards group-based assignment in every scenario.

Exam tip

"Who can do what" is always RBAC. Remember that permissions only add up, so the exam's least-privilege questions are really scope questions: pick the narrowest scope and the weakest role that still gets the job done. And if a scenario mentions temporary privileged access with approval, that is PIM.

Key takeaways

  • A role assignment is principal plus role definition plus scope, and scopes inherit downward.
  • Permissions are additive; there are no user-created deny assignments.
  • Assign roles to groups, not individuals, and choose the narrowest scope that works.
  • Actions cover the control plane; DataActions cover the data plane.
  • Use PIM to make privileged access eligible and just-in-time instead of permanent.
