The concept

A budget watches your spending and shouts before it hurts. You set an amount, a reset period, and alert thresholds. When spending crosses a threshold, Azure notifies whoever you named.

The moving parts:

  • Scope. Budgets can sit on a billing account, a subscription, or a resource group. Scope it to what you want to watch: the Meridian-DevTest subscription gets its own budget, separate from prod.
  • Reset period. Monthly, quarterly, or annually. Monthly is the common choice.
  • Amount and dates. The spending cap, with a start date and an expiration date.
  • Alert conditions. Thresholds as a percentage of the budget, on actual cost ("we have spent 80 percent") or forecasted cost ("at this pace we will hit 100 percent"). Forecast alerts fire before the money is gone, which is why you set both.
  • Alert recipients. Email addresses, plus Azure roles like Owner and Contributor so the right people always get the mail.
  • Action groups. The step most people miss. An alert can trigger an action group: email, SMS, webhook, Logic App, or an automation runbook. This is how a budget *does* something instead of just sending mail.

The critical fact, and an exam favorite: budgets do not stop spending. They only notify. If the scenario says "prevent the dev subscription from spending more than 500 dollars," a budget alone is the wrong answer. The right answers involve action: an action group wired to automation that stops VMs, or Azure Policy denying expensive SKUs, or a resource lock plus process. Budgets are the smoke alarm, not the sprinkler.

Key vocabulary

  • Budget: a spending threshold on a scope, with a reset period and alert rules.
  • Threshold: the percentage of the budget that triggers an alert, for example 80 percent.
  • Actual vs forecasted cost: what you have spent versus what you are projected to spend by period end.
  • Action group: a notification and automation target: email, SMS, webhook, Logic App, or runbook.

Try it

Budgets and alerts are free. You will create 1, verify it, then delete it.

In the portal:

  1. Search for Cost Management, open it, and select Budgets in the left menu. Set the scope to your subscription.
  2. Click Add. Name it lab-monthly-50. Set reset period to Monthly, amount to 50, start date to today, expiration date to 1 year out.
  3. Under alert conditions, add 2 rows: 80 percent of actual cost, and 100 percent of forecasted cost. Set alert recipients to your own email address.
  4. Click Create, then open the budget and confirm both alert conditions are listed.

In the CLI: budget management through the classic consumption API is exposed under az consumption budget. Run az consumption budget --help in Cloud Shell to read the create options. The portal path above is the 1 to practice first.

Clean up: open the budget and click Delete. Confirm it is gone from the Budgets list.

Worked example: Meridian Health

Meridian-DevTest gets a 500 dollar monthly budget. Alerts: 80 percent of actual cost emails the dev team lead; 100 percent of forecasted cost fires an action group that posts to the team's Teams channel via webhook and runs an automation runbook tagging idle VMs for review. Last quarter the forecast alert fired on the 22nd. The team stopped 4 forgotten VMs the same day and finished the month at 380 dollars. The budget did not stop the spend. The people and the runbook did.

Check your understanding

  1. Your budget hits 100 percent. Does Azure automatically stop your virtual machines?
  2. What is the difference between an alert on actual cost and an alert on forecasted cost?
  3. Which 3 reset periods can a budget use?
  4. How do you make a budget take action instead of just sending email?

Exam tip

"Budgets enforce spending limits" is always wrong. Budgets notify; enforcement comes from action groups wired to automation, from Azure Policy denying costly SKUs, or from process. When a question asks how to *prevent* overspend, look past the budget answer choice.

Key takeaways

  • Budgets watch a scope (billing account, subscription, resource group) over a monthly, quarterly, or annual period.
  • Set alerts on both actual cost (what you spent) and forecasted cost (where you are heading).
  • Alert recipients can be email addresses or Azure roles.
  • Action groups turn alerts into action: email, SMS, webhook, Logic App, runbook.
  • Budgets never stop spending on their own. They are the smoke alarm, not the sprinkler.
